\title{Calibration Budgets Do Not Transport Across P300 Speller Protocols: A Cross-Cohort Analysis of bigP3BCI}

\author{\IEEEauthorblockN{Devansh Sati}
\IEEEauthorblockA{\textit{Department / Institution} \\
\textit{City, Country} \\
devanshjsati@gmail.com}}

\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
P300 spellers need subject-specific calibration before use, and methods that shorten calibration are usually validated on a single dataset. We asked whether the calibration budget a P300 speller needs is a stable property of the decoder or varies across recording protocols and cohorts. Using 14 eligible cohorts of the public bigP3BCI archive (224 participants, 566 records), we trained a fixed shrinkage-LDA decoder on the first $k$ calibration characters in recording order and decoded all test characters offline. The budget B80 is defined as the smallest $k$ at which 80\% of a cohort's participants reach 70\% character accuracy. We estimated B80 where offline decoding reproduced online accuracy, and a co-primary budget based on flash-level AUC $\geq 0.75$ in all cohorts. Budgets ranged from 2 to 30 characters, a 15-fold spread under both definitions and a 10--18-fold spread across ten robustness variants. Learning-curve slopes were heterogeneous ($I^2=0.88$). No single budget brought 80\% of all participants to usable accuracy, and a budget borrowed from other cohorts missed a withheld cohort's budget by at least two characters in every cohort (median error 12--13 characters). Artefact rejection rate was the only cohort descriptor associated with slower learning after Holm correction ($p=0.016$). A calibration length validated on one P300 dataset should not be reused on another; budgets should be set per protocol and site and reported with recording quality.
\end{abstract}

\begin{IEEEkeywords}
brain--computer interface, P300 speller, calibration, EEG, transfer, reproducibility

\section{Introduction}
P300 spellers let people with severe motor impairment, including amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS), communicate by attending to flashing characters \cite{farwell1988,sellers2006}. Each new user must first copy-spell while labelled EEG trains a classifier. Calibration costs time and adds fatigue, so shortening it is a central goal of the field.

Most work proposes a method and shows that it needs less calibration on one dataset. Self-supervised pretraining with P300 aggregation, for example, was reported to cut calibration by 60\% on BCI Competition II-B \cite{hong2025}; transfer and semi-supervised methods make similar single-dataset claims \cite{gao2023,chen2026}. A budget found sufficient in one setting is then implicitly treated as sufficient elsewhere, although P300 protocols differ in grid size, flash paradigm, timing, electrodes, test procedure and population.

Gorenshtein et al.\ recently showed on bigP3BCI that the mapping from calibration-data quality to online accuracy does not transport across cohorts \cite{gorenshtein2026}. Their analysis always used the full calibration block, which leaves open how \emph{much} calibration is needed and whether that amount transports. We address this question. Our contributions are: (i) calibration learning curves for a fixed reference decoder in 14 cohorts, with calibration truncated strictly in recording order; (ii) per-cohort budgets based on character accuracy and on test-length-independent AUC, with bootstrap intervals; (iii) estimates of between-cohort heterogeneity, its moderators and leave-one-cohort-out transportability; and (iv) open code that reproduces every result.

\section{Related Work}
\subsection{Reducing calibration}
Approaches include subject-independent and self-trained CNNs \cite{gao2023}, semi-supervised adaptation \cite{chen2026}, self-supervised pretraining \cite{hong2025} and source-sample selection for transfer \cite{kilani2024}. Each is evaluated on one or a few datasets, and none measures how the required budget varies across protocols.

\subsection{Cross-dataset P300 analysis}
MOABB benchmarks ERP pipelines at a fixed training size \cite{moabb2024}; signal alignment improves classifier transfer across P300 datasets \cite{song2024}; and merging public P300 datasets has been discussed \cite{bianchi2022}. These works concern classifier transfer, not the subject-specific calibration a new cohort needs.

\subsection{Predicting performance from calibration}
Detectability indices predict P300 speller accuracy within a study \cite{mainsah2016}. Gorenshtein et al.\ found such mappings to be cohort-specific \cite{gorenshtein2026}. They asked whether a calibration \emph{score} transports; we ask whether a calibration \emph{budget} does.

\section{Data}
\subsection{Archive}
We used bigP3BCI v1.0.0 \cite{bigp3bci}: 20 visual P300 speller studies recorded in BCI2000 at Duke University and East Tennessee State University, comprising 6{,}980 EDF+ files with a shared 16-channel montage at 256\,Hz. Each session has a calibration (Train) phase without feedback and a Test phase with online feedback. Per-character flag channels record which characters each flash lit, which allows character decoding for every flash paradigm.

\subsection{Eligibility}
The unit of analysis is a participant\,$\times$\,session\,$\times$\,condition record with its own Train and Test files. Studies C and P were excluded because their online outcomes cannot be recovered \cite{gorenshtein2026}. Studies D, F, G and H were excluded because calibration and testing were recorded in different conditions. Cohorts with at least 5 participants and 4 evaluable budget levels were eligible, giving 14 cohorts (Table~\ref{tab:cohorts}).

\section{Methods}
\subsection{Preprocessing}
The 16 shared channels were band-pass filtered (0.5--30\,Hz, 4th-order zero-phase Butterworth) and epoched from $-200$ to 800\,ms around each flash. Epochs were baseline-corrected and decimated to 64\,Hz, giving 832 features per epoch. Calibration epochs exceeding 150\,$\mu$V were excluded from training; test epochs were decoded as recorded.

\subsection{Calibration truncation and decoding}
For each record and each $k\in\{1,2,3,4,6,8,10,12,15,20,25,30,\dots,\text{full}\}$, a decoder was trained only on the first $k$ calibration characters in recording order, so smaller budgets are strict subsets of larger ones. Features were standardised on calibration data and classified with shrinkage LDA (Ledoit--Wolf). Each test character was decoded as the character with the highest mean classifier score over the flashes that lit it, using all flashes the original protocol presented. With full calibration, offline accuracy reproduced online accuracy in pilot sessions (0.94 vs.\ 0.92; 0.93 vs.\ 0.93). Shuffled calibration labels gave chance-level accuracy in every cohort (0.01--0.04).

\subsection{Calibration budgets}
Participant accuracy at $k$ was pooled over that participant's records. A budget $k$ was evaluable when at least 80\% of a cohort's participants had data at $k$. B80 is the smallest evaluable $k$ at which at least 80\% of participants reach 70\% character accuracy, right-censored if none qualifies. In five cohorts (E, I, J, K, M), tests used dynamic stopping and offline accuracy fell more than 0.10 below online accuracy, so B80 is interpreted in the 9 cohorts with valid decoding. The co-primary AUC budget applies the same rule to flash-level AUC $\geq 0.75$, which does not depend on test length, in all 14 cohorts. Intervals came from 2{,}000 participant-cluster bootstrap resamples.

\subsection{Statistical analysis}
Stage~1 fitted, per cohort, a binomial GLM of correct characters on $\log k$ (or OLS of AUC on $\log k$), centred at $k=4$, with participant-clustered standard errors. Stage~2 pooled cohort slopes and intercepts with DerSimonian--Laird random effects, reporting $\tau$, $I^2$, a Hartung--Knapp confidence interval and a prediction interval. Eight descriptors (seconds per calibration character, test flashes per character, grid size, inter-stimulus interval, row--column share, characters lit per flash, ALS cohort, artefact rejection rate) entered one at a time in a Knapp--Hartung meta-regression of slopes, with Holm correction. For transportability, each cohort was withheld in turn and its budget predicted from the remaining cohorts' pooled participants (primary) or from the median of their budgets (secondary). The stage-2 code recovered a known moderator effect, and no spurious one, on simulated data.

\subsection{Learning curves differ between cohorts}
Accuracy rose with $k$ in every cohort, but at different rates and to different levels (Fig.~\ref{fig:curves}). In valid cohorts the pooled slope was 1.06 log-odds per $\log k$ (95\% CI 0.83--1.28), with between-cohort heterogeneity ($\tau=0.20$, $I^2=0.88$, $p<0.001$); the 95\% prediction interval for a new cohort was 0.55--1.56. Intercepts varied more ($\tau=0.98$, $I^2=0.96$). On AUC across all 14 cohorts the pooled slope was 0.067 per $\log k$ ($I^2=0.84$).

\subsection{Calibration budgets vary about 15-fold}
B80 ranged from 2 characters (S1) to 30 (Q) among the 7 valid cohorts that reached it, a 15-fold spread; A and N did not reach it. The AUC budget gave the same picture across all 14 cohorts, ranging from 2 to 30 characters (15-fold) among the 8 that reached it, and ranked cohorts similarly (Fig.~\ref{fig:budgets}). In time, the AUC budget ranged from 45\,s (S1) to 900\,s (B). Four dynamic-stopping cohorts (I, J, K, M) reached neither budget, even at an AUC threshold of 0.70.

\subsection{Recording quality, not protocol design, explains slope differences}
Artefact rejection rate was the only descriptor associated with the AUC learning-curve slope after Holm correction ($\beta=-0.012$ per SD, $p=0.002$, Holm $p=0.016$): cohorts with noisier calibration data learned more slowly (Fig.~\ref{fig:mods}). In valid cohorts, grid size, characters lit per flash and ALS status each explained about 38\% of slope variance ($p\approx0.03$, Holm $p=0.24$). These descriptors are collinear, since the ALS cohorts also used 36-character grids, so they cannot be separated. Test length, inter-stimulus interval and row--column share were not associated with slope.

\subsection{Budgets do not transport}
Pooling all other cohorts' participants never produced a budget at which 80\% of them were usable, so the primary predictor was undefined for every withheld cohort: no single calibration length served 80\% of users across protocols. The median budget of the other cohorts missed the withheld AUC budget by at least two characters in all 14 cohorts (median error 12; Fig.~\ref{fig:loco}) and missed B80 by at least two characters in all 9 valid cohorts (median 13). It overestimated budgets for fast cohorts (R, S1, S2) and underestimated them for slow ones (A, B, Q).

\section{Discussion}
\subsection{Principal findings}
With one fixed decoder and one fixed rule, the calibration at which 80\% of users reached usable accuracy ranged from 2 to 30 characters, or from under a minute to 15 minutes. A budget estimated elsewhere was wrong by a median of 12--13 characters, and no single budget served 80\% of all participants.

\subsection{Implications}
For method papers, this means a claim such as ``60\% less calibration'' is relative to a baseline that itself differs severalfold between datasets; the same absolute saving can be decisive in a slow cohort and irrelevant in a fast one. Calibration-reduction methods should be evaluated on several cohorts with per-cohort budgets. For deployment, calibration lengths copied from the literature will over-calibrate users in fast settings and under-calibrate them in slow ones, so budgets should be set per protocol and site, or adaptively. Recording quality was the only descriptor that survived correction, and removing noisy records moved a cohort from censored to a finite budget; reporting artefact rates alongside calibration results would make budgets comparable. This agrees with Gorenshtein et al., who found cohort-specific mappings only partly explained by protocol \cite{gorenshtein2026}.

\subsection{Limitations}
The analysis is offline with a fixed decoder rather than the original online classifier, whose BCI2000 parameters are not public. In five dynamic-stopping cohorts our decoder fell well below online accuracy, so B80 was restricted to valid cohorts and an AUC budget was added. Protocol, laboratory, hardware and population are nested within cohorts, so associations are not causal and ALS effects cannot be separated from grid size or timing. Cohorts with only 18 calibration characters censor larger budgets, so the true spread may exceed 15-fold. Finally, we tested one decoder; deep or transfer-learned decoders may lower budgets, and whether they remove between-cohort heterogeneity is left to future work.

\section{Conclusion}
Across 14 cohorts and 224 participants, the calibration budget for a fixed P300 decoder varied about 15-fold, did not transport between cohorts, and was associated with recording quality more than with protocol design. Calibration-reduction studies should report per-cohort budgets, and deployments should set calibration per protocol and site. Code reproducing all results from bigP3BCI v1.0.0 is available at \url{https://github.com/<username>/p300-calibration-budget}.
